Body Changes

15 Puberty Myths vs Facts

Fifteen common puberty myths about periods, growth, hygiene, skin and emotions, corrected with clear facts for teens and parents to share together.

By the Puberty101 Editorial TeamUpdated October 20266 min readFor Teens & Parents

Puberty comes with plenty of advice from friends, older siblings, and the internet, and not all of it is accurate. Some myths cause needless worry, while others lead to poor health habits. Here are 15 of the most common misconceptions, with the facts to set them straight.

Key takeaways

  • Puberty starts and progresses at different times for everyone, and that range is normal.
  • Periods are a healthy body process; they do not stop you from swimming, exercising, or showering.
  • Acne is mainly caused by hormones, not by dirt or poor hygiene.
  • Mood swings are real and linked to brain development and hormones.
  • When in doubt, check with a doctor, school nurse, or trusted health source.

Myths about timing and development

Myth 1: Everyone starts puberty at the same age

Fact: Puberty typically begins between about 8 and 13 in girls and 9 and 14 in boys. Genetics play a big role, so children often follow a similar timeline to their parents. Being early or late within this range is completely normal.

Myth 2: Puberty only affects your body

Fact: Puberty changes how you think and feel, too. The brain goes through major development during the teen years, affecting emotions, decision-making, and sleep patterns.

Myth 15: If you haven't started by 12, something is wrong

Fact: Many healthy young people start later than their friends. Doctors generally suggest a check-up if there are no signs of puberty by 13 in girls or 14 in boys, or if puberty starts before 8 in girls or 9 in boys.

Myths about periods

Myth 3: Only girls need to learn about periods

Fact: Understanding periods helps everyone be kinder and more supportive classmates, friends, and family members. Learning about all bodies reduces teasing and embarrassment.

Myth 4: You can't swim on your period

Fact: You can swim safely. Tampons, menstrual cups, and period swimwear are designed for water. Pads aren't suitable for swimming because they absorb water.

Myth 5: Exercise makes period cramps worse

Fact: Gentle exercise such as walking, cycling, or stretching often reduces cramps and boosts mood, thanks to improved blood flow and the release of feel-good chemicals.

Myth 6: Periods should be exactly 28 days apart

Fact: Cycles between about 21 and 45 days are common in teens, and periods are often irregular for the first year or two. Tracking periods on a calendar helps spot your own pattern.

Myth 7: Tampons can get lost inside you

Fact: The vagina is closed at the top by the cervix, which has only a tiny opening. A tampon cannot travel elsewhere in the body. Always use the lowest absorbency needed and change tampons regularly.

Myths about skin, hair and hygiene

Myth 8: Acne means you're dirty

Fact: Acne is mostly caused by hormones that increase oil production, combined with blocked pores and bacteria. Gentle washing twice a day helps, but scrubbing hard can make skin worse. A doctor or pharmacist can recommend treatments if acne is bothering you.

Myth 9: Shaving makes hair grow back thicker

Fact: Shaving cuts hair at the surface, leaving a blunt tip that may feel coarser, but it does not change the thickness, colour, or speed of growth. Whether to remove body hair is a personal choice.

Myths about growth and bodies

Myth 10: Growth spurts are all about the legs

Fact: Hands and feet often grow first, followed by arms and legs, then the torso. This can make teens feel temporarily clumsy, which is normal and passes.

Myth 11: Breast lumps in boys mean something is wrong

Fact: Many boys develop a small amount of breast tissue during puberty, sometimes on one side. It is usually caused by normal hormone changes and often fades within a couple of years. A doctor can check if it is painful or worrying.

Myth 12: Wet dreams mean something is wrong

Fact: Wet dreams, when semen is released during sleep, are a normal and involuntary part of puberty for boys. They are not a sign of illness, and not everyone has them.

Myths about feelings and wellbeing

Myth 13: Mood swings are just "attitude"

Fact: Hormonal changes and a developing brain can make emotions feel more intense. Understanding this helps teens and parents respond with patience. Ongoing sadness, worry, or withdrawal is different, and it is worth talking to a doctor or counsellor.

Myth 14: Teens need less sleep than kids

Fact: Teens need about 8 to 10 hours of sleep each night. Body clocks naturally shift later during puberty, so a consistent bedtime routine and limiting screens before bed really help.

How to spot a myth

When you hear a new claim about puberty, ask:

  • Where did this information come from?
  • Is it from a recognised health organisation or a qualified professional?
  • Does it make me feel scared or ashamed? Reliable health information rarely does.

The bottom line

Puberty is full of changes, and myths can make them seem stranger or scarier than they are. Most of what happens is normal, healthy, and shared by millions of young people. If something does not sound right, check with a trusted adult or health professional.

Frequently asked questions

Is it true that everyone starts puberty at the same age?

No. Puberty usually starts between about 8 and 13 for girls and 9 and 14 for boys, and starting earlier or later within that range is normal.

Does eating chocolate cause acne?

There is no strong evidence that chocolate alone causes acne. Hormones are the main driver, though some research suggests diet may play a small role for some people.

Can you exercise during your period?

Yes. Exercise is safe during a period and can even ease cramps and improve mood.
