Teaching Puberty in the Classroom: A Practical Guide for Educators

A practical guide for teachers on puberty lessons: lesson structure, ground rules, an anonymous question box, inclusive language and parent communication.

By the Puberty101 Editorial TeamUpdated October 20266 min readFor Educators

Teaching puberty can feel daunting, even for experienced educators. With clear structure, firm ground rules, and a few proven tools, these lessons often become some of the most valued in the school year. This guide covers how to plan, deliver, and follow up on puberty education with confidence.

Key takeaways

  • Teach puberty before most students start changing, typically from around ages 8 to 10.
  • Set ground rules together at the start of every session to build a safe, respectful space.
  • An anonymous question box lets every student ask what they really want to know.
  • Use accurate, inclusive language and teach changes in all bodies to all students.
  • Inform parents early, share materials, and invite them to continue the conversation at home.

Before you teach: preparation

Good puberty lessons start well before you enter the classroom.

  • Know your curriculum and policy. Check what your school, district, or national curriculum requires and any rules on parental notification.
  • Check your comfort level. Practise saying anatomical terms aloud until they feel routine. Students take their cue from your tone.
  • Gather reliable resources. Use materials from recognised health organisations, and preview every video or handout.
  • Plan for safeguarding. Know your school's designated safeguarding or child protection lead and how to report a concern.

Setting ground rules

Ground rules create a sense of safety. Build them with your students at the start of the first session, write them up, and revisit them each time.

Useful ground rules include:

  • We listen respectfully and do not laugh at anyone's question
  • We use correct names for body parts
  • No personal questions to anyone, including the teacher
  • We do not share personal stories about others
  • It is okay to pass or to feel embarrassed
  • What we share here is respected, but teachers must act if someone's safety is at risk

That last rule is important. Be clear from the start that you cannot keep a secret if a student tells you something suggesting they are unsafe.

A sample lesson structure

A clear structure keeps lessons focused and predictable. Here is a simple framework for a 45 to 60 minute session.

Stage Time Purpose
Welcome and ground rules 5 min Revisit rules, set a calm tone
Warm-up 5 to 10 min Gauge prior knowledge with a quick activity
Core teaching 15 to 20 min Introduce new content with clear visuals
Activity 10 to 15 min Apply learning through sorting, matching, or scenarios
Question box 5 to 10 min Answer anonymous questions
Reflection 5 min Summarise, signpost support, and close

Warm-up ideas

  • A "true or false" quiz on common puberty myths
  • Sorting cards of changes into "physical" and "emotional"
  • A quick brainstorm of "things that change as we grow up"

Core teaching tips

Use simple, labelled diagrams rather than photographs. Keep explanations short and factual. Normalise variation by repeating that everyone develops at their own pace.

Activity ideas

  • Scenario cards, such as "A friend is worried their voice keeps cracking. What could you say?"
  • Matching hygiene products to their uses
  • Timelines showing that puberty can start anywhere from about 8 to 14

Using an anonymous question box

The question box is the single most useful tool in puberty education. Students often have real worries they would never ask out loud.

How to run it

  1. Give every student a slip of paper and ask everyone to write something, even "no question," so nobody stands out.
  2. Collect slips in a box or envelope.
  3. Read questions privately before answering, ideally at the end of the lesson or the start of the next one.
  4. Group similar questions and answer them calmly and factually.

Sorting questions

Type How to respond
Factual and in scope Answer clearly and briefly
Outside the lesson's scope Acknowledge it, and suggest talking with a parent, caregiver, or school nurse
Intended to shock Answer the underlying factual point if appropriate, or set it aside without comment
Raises a safeguarding concern Do not read it aloud; follow your school's safeguarding procedure

It is perfectly fine to say, "Good question. I'll find out and get back to you."

Inclusive language and teaching

Inclusive teaching means every student sees themselves in the lesson and learns to respect others.

  • Teach all changes to all students. Everyone benefits from understanding periods, voice changes, and other developments, whatever their own body is doing.
  • Use precise, neutral phrasing where it fits, such as "people who have periods" alongside clear anatomical terms, following your school's guidance.
  • Represent a range of bodies. Choose resources showing different sizes, skin tones, and abilities.
  • Consider additional needs. Provide visual supports, simplified handouts, or pre-teaching for students who need them.
  • Be mindful of culture and faith. Families hold different values; keep content factual and respectful.

Communicating with parents

Parents are partners in puberty education. Strong communication prevents surprises and encourages conversations at home.

  • Send a letter in advance explaining what will be taught, when, and why.
  • Share materials or offer a preview session where parents can see resources.
  • Explain any withdrawal rights according to your local policy.
  • Suggest follow-up questions families can use at home.
  • Provide a contact for parents who have concerns or want extra guidance.

Many parents feel relieved when school opens the door, and they appreciate prompts for starting the conversation themselves.

After the lesson

  • Remind students where to find support, such as the school nurse or counsellor
  • Keep a supply of period products available discreetly
  • Note topics that caused confusion and revisit them
  • Reflect on what worked and adjust for next time

The bottom line

Puberty education works best when it is planned, predictable, and respectful. Clear ground rules, a well-run question box, inclusive language, and strong links with parents help students feel safe enough to learn. Delivered with calm confidence, these lessons give young people knowledge they will use for life.

Frequently asked questions

Should puberty lessons be taught in mixed or separate groups?

Most guidance recommends teaching the core content to everyone together so all students understand changes in all bodies. Some schools add optional small-group sessions for practical questions.

How should I handle a question I am not allowed to answer?

Acknowledge it respectfully, explain that it is outside today's lesson, and suggest the student talk with a parent, caregiver, or school nurse. Follow your school's policy.

What age should puberty education begin?

Many curricula introduce puberty around ages 8 to 10, before most students begin changing, with topics revisited in more depth in later grades.
