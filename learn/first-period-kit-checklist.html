How to Build a First-Period Kit: A Parent and Teen Checklist

A simple checklist for building a first-period kit for school or bag, with the best products for beginners, packing tips and what to do in a leak.

By the Puberty101 Editorial TeamUpdated October 20266 min readFor Teens & Parents

Nobody can predict exactly when a first period will arrive, which is why a small, ready-to-go kit is one of the most reassuring things a young person can carry. A good kit turns a potentially stressful surprise into a minor, manageable moment. Here is how to build one together.

Key takeaways

  • First periods usually start between ages 10 and 15, often about two years after breasts begin to develop.
  • A basic kit needs pads, spare underwear, wipes, and a discreet pouch.
  • Pads and period underwear are the simplest products for beginners.
  • Build the kit together so your child knows how to use everything inside it.
  • Restock the kit after every use and check it at the start of each school term.

When to start carrying a kit

Most people get their first period between 10 and 15, though anywhere from 8 to 16 can be normal. Helpful clues that a period may be coming within the next several months include:

  • Breasts have been developing for around one to two years
  • Pubic and underarm hair have appeared
  • White or clear discharge is showing up in underwear
  • A recent growth spurt

If any of these signs are present, it is a good time to put a kit together. Carrying one early does no harm, and it builds confidence.

The first-period kit checklist

Aim for something small enough to fit in a school bag, sports bag, or locker.

The essentials

  • [ ] 2 to 3 pads (regular absorbency, individually wrapped)
  • [ ] 1 to 2 panty liners for light days or spotting
  • [ ] 1 pair of clean, dark-coloured underwear
  • [ ] A small pack of unscented wipes
  • [ ] A sealable bag for storing used underwear or clothing
  • [ ] A discreet zip pouch to hold everything

Helpful extras

  • [ ] Period underwear as a backup or alternative
  • [ ] A small hand sanitiser
  • [ ] A spare pair of leggings or shorts, or a sweater to tie around the waist
  • [ ] A note card with simple reminders on changing a pad
  • [ ] Pain relief, only if your school allows it and an adult has approved the right dose

Check your school's rules on medication before packing anything. Many schools require medicines to be kept with the school nurse or office.

Choosing products for beginners

Every product below is safe, and the right choice depends on comfort and preference.

Product How it works Good for beginners? Change about every
Pads Sticky strip attaches to underwear and absorbs flow Yes, easiest to start with 3 to 4 hours, or sooner if full
Panty liners Thin pads for very light flow Yes, for spotting or backup 3 to 4 hours
Period underwear Absorbent layers built into washable underwear Yes, very comfortable Up to 12 hours, depending on flow
Tampons Inserted into the vagina to absorb flow Possible with guidance, often later 4 to 8 hours, never longer
Menstrual cups Reusable silicone cup that collects flow Usually tried later Up to 12 hours

For a first period, most families start with pads or period underwear. Tampons and cups are safe options to explore later if a teen wants to, especially for swimming or sports. Anyone using tampons should choose the lowest absorbency needed and change them regularly to reduce the rare risk of toxic shock syndrome.

Build the kit together

Making the kit as a team is a natural way to talk about periods without a formal talk. Try these steps:

  1. Shop together or browse products at home so your child can see and feel the options.
  2. Open a pad and show how to unwrap it, stick it to underwear, and fold up the used one in its wrapper.
  3. Practise at home so the first time is not at school.
  4. Explain disposal. Used pads go in the bin, never the toilet.
  5. Let them choose the pouch, colours, or brands. Ownership makes them more likely to carry it.

What to do if a period arrives unexpectedly

Leaks happen to almost everyone at some point, so it helps to have a plan.

  • Head to the bathroom and use a pad from the kit.
  • If blood has reached clothing, change into the spare underwear and tie a sweater around the waist if needed.
  • If there is no kit, ask the school nurse, office, a trusted teacher, or a friend. Staff are used to these requests.
  • At home, rinse stained clothing in cold water as soon as possible.

Remind your child that a first period is often light and may look brown or pink rather than bright red. That is completely normal.

Keeping the kit ready

  • Restock right after the kit is used.
  • Check expiry-free items like wipes for dryness each term.
  • Keep a second kit in a sports bag or locker if your child is very active.
  • Track periods on a calendar or app once they start, so the next one is less of a surprise.

Early periods are often irregular for the first year or two, so carrying a kit every day remains a good habit.

When to speak to a doctor

Contact a doctor if periods start before age 8, have not started by 15, soak through a pad every hour or two, last longer than seven days, or come with pain that stops normal activities.

The bottom line

A first-period kit is small, inexpensive, and incredibly reassuring. Build it together, practise with the products at home, and keep it stocked. With a plan in place, a first period becomes just another part of growing up rather than a moment to dread.

Frequently asked questions

When should my child start carrying a period kit?

Once breast development has been underway for about a year or two, or once discharge appears in underwear, it is a good time to start carrying a kit, since a first period often follows within months.

What is the best period product for a first period?

Pads are usually the easiest place to start because they are simple to use and require no insertion. Period underwear is another comfortable option for beginners.

What should my child do if they get their period at school without a kit?

They can ask the school nurse, office, a teacher, or a trusted friend for a pad. Many schools keep spare supplies and are used to helping students discreetly.
