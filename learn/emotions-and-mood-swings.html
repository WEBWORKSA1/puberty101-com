Feelings & Mind

Hormones and Mood Swings: Understanding Your Feelings During Puberty

Why emotions can feel bigger during puberty, how hormones and brain growth play a role, practical ways to cope and when to reach out for support.

By the Puberty101 Editorial TeamUpdated October 20266 min readFor Teens & Parents

One minute you feel fine, and the next you are close to tears or snapping at someone you care about. If your feelings seem louder and faster than they used to be, you are not imagining it. Puberty changes your body and your brain, and that can make emotions feel more intense for a while.

Key takeaways

  • Mood swings are a common and normal part of puberty.
  • Hormone changes, brain development, sleep and stress all affect how you feel.
  • Simple tools like naming feelings, moving your body and getting enough sleep can help.
  • Talking to someone you trust is one of the strongest ways to cope.
  • If low mood or worry lasts two weeks or more, or feels overwhelming, reach out for help.

Why feelings get bigger during puberty

Hormones

During puberty, your body starts making more of certain hormones, including estrogen, progesterone and testosterone. These chemical messengers guide physical changes, but they also interact with parts of the brain that handle emotion. As levels rise and shift, your mood can shift too.

A brain under construction

Your brain keeps developing well into your twenties. The emotional centre of the brain becomes very active during the teen years, while the area behind your forehead, which helps with planning, calming down and thinking things through, is still maturing. This mismatch is one reason feelings can arrive quickly and strongly before the "pause and think" part catches up.

Everything else changing at once

Puberty often overlaps with new schools, harder classes, shifting friendships and more independence. On top of that, your body is changing in ways you may feel unsure about. It makes sense that all of this can feel like a lot.

Common feelings during puberty

You might notice:

  • Feeling irritable or easily annoyed
  • Crying more easily than before
  • Wanting more privacy or time alone
  • Feeling self-conscious about how you look
  • Worrying more about what others think
  • Feeling excited, confident and energetic one day and flat the next

None of these make you "dramatic" or "too sensitive." They are human responses to a period of big change.

Practical ways to cope with big feelings

Name it

Putting a word to what you feel, such as "frustrated," "embarrassed" or "nervous," can make the feeling easier to manage. Some people find it helps to write feelings down in a notebook.

Calm your body

When emotions spike, your body often reacts with a faster heartbeat or tight muscles. Try:

  • Slow breathing: breathe in for a count of four, hold for a moment, and breathe out for a count of six.
  • Stepping outside or changing rooms for a few minutes.
  • Splashing cool water on your face.
  • Stretching or going for a short walk.

Move every day

Physical activity releases chemicals in the brain that help lift mood and ease stress. It does not have to be a sport. Dancing, biking, walking the dog or shooting hoops all count.

Protect your sleep

Most teens need 8 to 10 hours of sleep each night. When you are tired, everything feels harder and emotions run hotter. Keeping a regular bedtime and putting screens away before sleep can make a noticeable difference.

Give yourself a reset

If you said something you regret during a tough moment, it is okay. A simple "Sorry, I was having a hard time" can repair a lot. Everyone has off days. Being able to own a mistake and move forward is a sign of growing maturity, and it gets easier with practice. Be as patient with yourself as you would be with a good friend.

For parents: supporting a teen through mood changes

  • Listen first. Teens often want to be heard before they want advice.
  • Stay steady. A calm response helps your teen calm down too.
  • Keep routines. Regular meals, sleep and family time give structure during a changing time.
  • Respect privacy, stay connected. Wanting more space is normal, but keep checking in.
  • Notice patterns. Pay attention if changes last a long time or seem extreme.

When to talk to a doctor or trusted adult

Ups and downs are expected, but some signs suggest it is time for extra support. Talk to a parent, school counsellor, doctor or another trusted adult if you notice:

  • Feeling sad, hopeless, empty or worried most days for two weeks or more
  • Losing interest in things you used to enjoy
  • Big changes in sleep or appetite
  • Pulling away from friends and family
  • Trouble concentrating or keeping up at school
  • Thoughts of hurting yourself, or feeling like you cannot go on

Feelings like these are not a sign of weakness, and they can get better with help. A doctor can check for things like anxiety or depression and connect you with support that works.

If you ever feel unsafe or overwhelmed, please reach out right away to a trusted adult. Free, confidential helplines are also available in many places, such as 988 in the US, Kids Help Phone in Canada and Childline in the UK, and you can find one wherever you live. In an emergency, contact your local emergency number.

The bottom line

Stronger, faster-changing emotions are a normal part of puberty, driven by hormones, a growing brain and lots of life changes at once. Sleep, movement, healthy routines and honest conversations can make the ride smoother. You never have to handle hard feelings alone, so reach out to someone you trust whenever you need to.

Frequently asked questions

Are mood swings during puberty normal?

Yes. Changing hormone levels and a fast-developing brain can make emotions feel stronger and shift more quickly, and this is a common part of growing up.

How can I tell if it is more than normal moodiness?

If low mood, worry or irritability lasts most days for two weeks or more, or gets in the way of school, sleep, eating or friendships, it is a good idea to talk to a trusted adult or doctor.

How can parents support a moody teen?

Stay calm, listen more than you lecture, and keep routines like regular meals and sleep in place. Let your teen know they can come to you without being judged.

Does sleep affect mood?

Very much. Not getting enough sleep makes it harder to manage stress and emotions, so a regular sleep schedule is one of the most helpful things you can do.
