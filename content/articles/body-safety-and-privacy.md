Puberty is a time when children become more aware of their bodies and more independent in the world. It is also an ideal moment to strengthen the safety lessons that protect them. Teaching body autonomy, privacy, and boundaries in a calm, everyday way helps children feel confident and gives them the words and permission to speak up.

## Key takeaways

- Children have the right to decide who touches their body, and they should respect others' bodies too.
- Using correct anatomical names is a proven safety practice, not an embarrassing extra.
- Teach that surprises are fine but secrets about bodies or safety are never okay.
- Help children trust the feeling that something is wrong and know exactly who to tell.
- Your calm, open reaction is the most powerful protective tool you have.

## Why puberty is a key time for body safety

As children grow, they spend more time away from parents: at sports, sleepovers, clubs, and online. Their bodies are changing, and they may feel shy or confused about it. Clear safety lessons at this stage reinforce what they learned when younger and adapt it for new situations.

Body safety education is not about frightening children. It is about giving them knowledge and confidence, the same way we teach road safety or swimming.

## Core lessons to teach

### Body autonomy: "My body belongs to me"

Children should know they can say no to touch that makes them uncomfortable, even from relatives or people they like. You can model this at home by not forcing hugs or kisses and offering alternatives like a wave or high five.

Equally important, children learn to respect other people's no. Body autonomy works in both directions.

### Correct names for body parts

Using accurate words such as penis, vulva, vagina, testicles, and breasts gives children clear language. Child protection organisations recommend this because:

- Children can describe health problems accurately to a doctor
- It shows that bodies are not shameful topics
- Children can clearly explain if something has happened to them
- Adults hearing a disclosure can understand it immediately

### Privacy and private parts

Explain that private parts are the parts of the body covered by underwear or a swimsuit. Others should not touch or look at them, and children should not be asked to touch or look at anyone else's.

There are a few exceptions, and it helps to name them simply: a parent helping with hygiene when a child is very young, or a doctor during a check-up with a parent or caregiver present or aware. As children reach puberty, they can take over their own hygiene and should be given increasing privacy at home.

### Surprises versus secrets

This is one of the simplest and most protective rules a family can have.

| Surprises | Secrets |
|---|---|
| Make people happy | Can make someone feel worried, scared, or confused |
| Are shared soon | Are meant to be kept for a long time or forever |
| Example: a birthday gift | Example: anything about touching, bodies, photos, or being hurt |

Teach your child: "In our family, we don't keep secrets from trusted adults. You will never be in trouble for telling me something someone asked you to keep secret."

### Trusting the "uh-oh" feeling

Help children notice when something feels wrong, such as a tummy ache, racing heart, or the sense that they want to get away. Tell them that this feeling is worth listening to, even if they cannot explain why, and even if the person seems nice.

## Warning patterns, in brief

Without going into detail, it helps children to know that it is a warning sign if any person, adult or young person:

- Asks them to keep a secret, especially from parents
- Makes them feel uncomfortable about their body or touch
- Tries to spend lots of time alone with them or isolate them
- Sends or asks for photos of private parts, online or in person

The message is simple: if this happens, tell a trusted adult, and keep telling until someone helps.

## Building a trusted adult network

Ask your child to name three to five trusted adults they could go to, such as a parent, grandparent, teacher, school counsellor, or coach. Practise what they could say: "Something happened that made me feel uncomfortable, and I need to talk."

Remind them that if one adult does not listen, they should tell another.

## Online privacy and boundaries

Puberty often coincides with a first phone or more online time. Add these points to your safety talks:

- Never share photos of private parts, and never ask others for them
- Keep personal details like address and school private
- Block and report anyone who makes them uncomfortable
- Tell a trusted adult about any worrying message, without fear of losing their device

That last point matters. Children often stay silent because they fear punishment. Make it clear that telling you will never result in being in trouble.

## Keeping communication open

- **Talk often.** Weave safety into everyday chats rather than one serious lecture.
- **Use "what if" questions.** "What would you do if a friend showed you something online that felt wrong?"
- **Stay calm.** Children watch your reactions closely. Calm responses invite more honesty.
- **Praise telling.** Thank your child whenever they share something, big or small.
- **Respect their growing privacy.** Knock before entering, and let them handle their own hygiene.

## If your child tells you something

Stay calm, listen without pushing for details, and say: "Thank you for telling me. It's not your fault. I'm going to help." Avoid confronting anyone yourself. Contact a helpline or your local child protection service, and call emergency services if your child is in immediate danger.

**Helplines for advice and support:**

- United States: Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline, 1-800-422-4453
- Canada: Kids Help Phone, 1-800-668-6868
- United Kingdom: Childline (run by the NSPCC), 0800 1111

These services are free and confidential, and they can support both children and worried adults.

## The bottom line

Body safety is about confidence, not fear. When children know their bodies belong to them, use clear words, understand that secrets about safety are never okay, and trust you to listen calmly, they are far better protected. Keep the conversation going through puberty and beyond.
