Puberty brings change, and change can feel especially challenging for autistic and ADHD children. New sensations, routines, and social expectations can arrive all at once. With early preparation, clear visual supports, and sensory-friendly choices, you can help your child move through puberty with more confidence and less stress.

## Key takeaways

- Start early and repeat often, so changes feel expected rather than sudden.
- Visual schedules and checklists make new hygiene routines easier to follow.
- Social stories explain changes and expectations in a calm, predictable way.
- Sensory-friendly products and routines can reduce resistance to hygiene and periods.
- Concrete, literal language works better than hints or euphemisms.

## Why puberty can feel different for neurodivergent kids

Every child experiences puberty in their own way, but neurodivergent children may face particular challenges:

- **Change and predictability.** Autistic children often rely on routine, and puberty changes the body without warning.
- **Sensory differences.** New smells, body hair, sweat, or the feel of pads and deodorant can be uncomfortable or distressing.
- **Executive function.** Children with ADHD may find it hard to remember multi-step routines like showering, applying deodorant, and changing a pad.
- **Social understanding.** Unwritten rules about privacy and public versus private behaviour may need to be taught directly.
- **Emotional regulation.** Hormonal shifts can make big feelings harder to manage.

None of these mean your child cannot thrive. They simply mean support works best when it is planned and explicit.

## Start early and keep it concrete

Begin talking about puberty a year or two before you expect changes, often around age 7 or 8. Many neurodivergent children benefit from hearing the same information many times in the same way.

- Use correct anatomical names and plain, literal words.
- Avoid euphemisms like "Aunt Flo" or "becoming a woman," which can confuse.
- Explain what will happen, why it happens, and what to do about it.
- Use books, diagrams, and models so information is visual as well as spoken.

## Visual schedules and checklists

Visual supports turn abstract routines into clear, manageable steps. Picture-based or written checklists can be laminated and placed where the task happens, like the bathroom mirror or inside a wardrobe door.

### Sample morning hygiene checklist

- [ ] Use the toilet
- [ ] Wash hands
- [ ] Wash face with gentle cleanser
- [ ] Brush teeth for two minutes
- [ ] Put on deodorant
- [ ] Put on clean underwear and clothes
- [ ] Brush hair

### Sample period-change steps

- [ ] Wash hands
- [ ] Take off used pad and wrap it in paper or the new wrapper
- [ ] Put used pad in the bin
- [ ] Unwrap new pad and stick it to underwear
- [ ] Wash hands again

Timers, phone reminders, or alarms can help ADHD children remember routines throughout the day, such as changing a pad at lunchtime or applying deodorant after sport.

## Using social stories

A social story is a short, positive description of a situation, written from the child's perspective. It explains what will happen and what the child can do, using simple and reassuring language.

A social story about periods might include:

- What a period is, in simple terms
- That it is healthy and happens to many people
- What it might look and feel like
- Where supplies are kept and how to use them
- Who to tell if they need help
- That periods are private and talked about with trusted adults

You can write social stories for many puberty topics: body hair, sweating, wet dreams, using deodorant, changing for sport, or privacy at home. Read them often, well before the change happens, and keep them somewhere your child can return to.

## Sensory-friendly hygiene

Sensory sensitivities can make hygiene feel genuinely uncomfortable. Small changes often make a big difference.

| Challenge | Possible solutions |
|---|---|
| Dislikes the feel of showering | Adjust water pressure, try a handheld shower, or offer baths |
| Strong reaction to scents | Choose fragrance-free soaps, shampoos, and deodorants |
| Dislikes spray deodorant | Try a roll-on, stick, or cream instead |
| Sensitive to towel texture | Use soft, well-washed towels or a bathrobe |
| Finds hair washing distressing | Use a visor, washcloth over eyes, or a tear-free shampoo |
| Forgets steps | Use a visual checklist and consistent timing |

Let your child try products and choose what feels best. Ownership often reduces resistance.

## Sensory-friendly period products

For children who will have periods, practising with products before the first period arrives can prevent distress.

- **Period underwear** often feels most like ordinary underwear and has no sticky strips or crinkly wrappers.
- **Soft, fragrance-free pads** with a cotton top layer may suit those sensitive to synthetic textures.
- **Wings or no wings** is a personal preference; let your child test both.
- **Tampons and cups** may be uncomfortable for some and suit others later on.

Let your child wear a pad or period underwear at home for short periods before they need it, so the sensation feels familiar.

## Teaching privacy and boundaries

Neurodivergent children may need direct teaching about what is private and where. Clear rules help:

- Private body parts are kept covered in public places
- Changing clothes happens in a bedroom or bathroom with the door closed
- Talking about periods or body changes is for home or with trusted adults

Also teach body autonomy: your child can say no to unwanted touch, and should tell a trusted adult if anything feels wrong. Visual supports and role play can help these lessons stick.

## Supporting emotions

- Use emotion charts or scales to help your child name feelings
- Build in calm-down time after school or during periods
- Watch for changes in sleep, appetite, or behaviour
- Work with your child's doctor, school, or therapist if challenges grow

## The bottom line

Puberty can be smoother for autistic and ADHD children when it is predictable, visual, and sensory-aware. Start early, repeat often, offer clear checklists and social stories, and let your child help choose comfortable products. With patient, concrete support, your child can approach these changes with growing confidence.
