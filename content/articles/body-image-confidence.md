During puberty, your body can change so quickly that looking in the mirror feels like meeting someone new. It is completely normal to have mixed feelings about that. Body image is about how you see, think and feel about your body, and with some care it can grow stronger even while everything is changing.

## Key takeaways

- Changes in height, shape, skin and hair during puberty are normal and expected.
- Every body develops on its own timeline, so comparing yourself to others is rarely fair.
- Social media often shows edited or unrealistic images.
- Confidence grows from focusing on what your body can do and on who you are as a person.
- If thoughts about your body take over your day, talk to a trusted adult or doctor.

## Why body image can feel shaky during puberty

### Your body is changing fast

Puberty brings growth spurts, wider hips or shoulders, new body hair, skin changes and changes in body shape. Gaining some body fat and muscle is a healthy and necessary part of growing. These changes are signs your body is doing exactly what it is supposed to do.

### Different timelines

Some people start puberty early, some late, and most somewhere in between. You might be the tallest in your class one year and average the next. Being "ahead" or "behind" your friends can make you feel out of place, but it says nothing about whether your body is healthy.

### Comparison everywhere

Movies, ads and social media often show a very narrow idea of what bodies "should" look like. Many of these images are edited, filtered or posed under perfect lighting. Comparing your everyday self to someone else's highlight reel is not a fair match.

## Body image myths and facts

| Myth | Fact |
|---|---|
| There is one "ideal" body type. | Healthy bodies come in many shapes and sizes. |
| Everyone else is comfortable with their body. | Most teens feel unsure about their bodies at some point. |
| Photos online show what people really look like. | Many are edited, filtered or carefully chosen. |
| Changing your body will make you confident. | Confidence comes mostly from how you think and treat yourself. |

## Building confidence from the inside out

### Focus on what your body does

Your body lets you laugh with friends, play music, run, dance, hug the people you love and learn new things. Shifting attention from how your body looks to what it does for you can make a real difference in how you feel about it.

### Practise body neutrality

You do not have to love every part of your body every day. Body neutrality means treating your body with respect without needing to judge it as good or bad. A thought like "This is my body, and it carries me through the day" is a helpful place to start.

### Talk to yourself like a friend

Notice the way you speak to yourself. Would you say those words to a friend? If not, try swapping harsh thoughts for kinder ones:

- Instead of "I hate how I look," try "My body is changing, and that is okay."
- Instead of "Everyone looks better than me," try "I am comparing my real life to edited pictures."

### Curate your feed

- Unfollow or mute accounts that leave you feeling worse about yourself.
- Follow people with a range of body types, abilities and backgrounds.
- Follow accounts about hobbies, art, sports or learning rather than appearance.
- Take regular breaks from scrolling.

### Wear what feels comfortable

Growing bodies sometimes outgrow clothes quickly. Wearing clothes that fit well and feel good, rather than squeezing into old sizes, can boost comfort and confidence.

### Take care of yourself, not control yourself

Regular meals, enough sleep and activity you enjoy help you feel your best. These habits are about energy and health, not about changing how your body looks. Restricting food or exercising to punish your body is not healthy and can be harmful.

## Building confidence beyond appearance

Confidence is much bigger than looks. It also grows when you:

- Learn a new skill or improve at something you care about
- Help someone else or volunteer
- Spend time with people who make you feel accepted
- Stand up for what you believe in
- Celebrate small wins, like finishing a project or trying something new

## For parents and educators

Adults have a powerful influence on how young people see their bodies.

- Avoid comments about weight or appearance, including about your own body or other people's.
- Praise effort, kindness, creativity and character.
- Talk openly about how media images are edited and selected.
- Serve regular family meals without labelling foods as "good" or "bad."
- Encourage activity for fun and strength, not to change body shape.

## When to talk to a doctor or trusted adult

Feeling unsure about your body sometimes is normal. It may be time to reach out to a parent, school counsellor, doctor or another trusted adult if:

- Thoughts about your body or food take up a lot of your day
- You are skipping meals, eating in secret or feeling out of control around food
- You exercise even when sick or injured, or feel very anxious if you miss it
- You avoid school, friends or activities because of how you look
- You feel sad, anxious or hopeless most days

These can be signs that you need extra support, and help is available. If you ever feel overwhelmed or unsafe, talk to a trusted adult right away. Free, confidential helplines also exist in many places, such as 988 in the US, Kids Help Phone in Canada and Childline in the UK.

## The bottom line

Your body is growing and changing on its own schedule, and that is exactly how it should be. Comparison, social media and fast changes can make body image feel shaky, but kindness toward yourself, a healthy feed and a focus on what your body can do build lasting confidence. If your feelings about your body are weighing on you, reach out. You deserve support.
